2. Key ideas

The stated rate

Nominal interest rate

The rate written on a loan, bond, or savings account. It tells you how many more dollars you get back, without adjusting for what those dollars can buy.

r = i − π

Real interest rate

The nominal rate minus inflation. It tells you how much your purchasing power grows. A 5% nominal rate with 3% inflation is a 2% real rate. If inflation is higher than the nominal rate, the real rate is negative.

i = r + πᵉ

The Fisher equation

Lenders add expected inflation to the real return they want. When expected inflation rises, the nominal rate rises by the same amount and the expected real rate stays the same. This is the Fisher effect.

Before vs. after

Ex ante and ex post

The ex ante real rate is planned: nominal rate minus expected inflation. The ex post real rate is realized: nominal rate minus actual inflation. They differ only when inflation surprises people.

Redistribution

Unanticipated inflation

If inflation is higher than expected, borrowers repay with dollars that buy less, so borrowers gain and lenders lose. If inflation is lower than expected, lenders gain and borrowers lose. Anticipated inflation is already built into the nominal rate, so it moves no purchasing power between them.

Which graph

Two rates, two graphs

The money market graph has the nominal interest rate on its vertical axis. The loanable funds graph has the real interest rate. Using the wrong label costs a point on the FRQ.

4. On the FRQ

How to earn the points

  • Write the formula, then the numbers, then the answer: "real interest rate = nominal interest rate − inflation rate = 5% − 3% = 2%." Graders give credit for the work.
  • To find the nominal rate a lender should charge, add expected inflation to the real rate it wants: 4% + 3% = 7%.
  • Keep the sign. A real rate of −2% is a correct answer; say the lender's purchasing power falls.
  • For who gains, name the group and give the reason: "Borrowers gain because they repay the loan with dollars that have less purchasing power than expected."
  • Use the word unanticipated (or unexpected). Only inflation that differs from expectations redistributes purchasing power.
  • Label axes: money market uses "Nominal interest rate"; loanable funds uses "Real interest rate".

Common mistakes

  • Subtracting backward. Real rate = nominal − inflation, so 8% nominal and 3% inflation gives 5%, not −5%.
  • Saying "inflation always hurts lenders." Expected inflation is already in the nominal rate. Lenders lose only when inflation is higher than expected.
  • Mixing up who gains. Higher-than-expected inflation helps borrowers (debtors). Lower-than-expected inflation helps lenders (creditors and savers with fixed rates).
  • Using actual inflation for the rate a lender sets in advance. The lender cannot know actual inflation yet, so it uses expected inflation.
  • Dividing or multiplying. AP uses simple subtraction and addition for these questions.
  • Thinking a positive nominal rate means savers gain. If inflation is above the nominal rate, savers lose purchasing power.
