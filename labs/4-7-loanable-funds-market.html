Where the curves come from

Supply of loanable funds (SLF) comes from saving: private saving by households and firms, public saving when the government runs a surplus, and foreign saving that flows in. It slopes up because a higher real interest rate rewards saving.

Demand for loanable funds (DLF) comes from borrowers: firms that want to invest in capital, and the government when it runs a deficit. It slopes down because fewer investment projects pay off at a higher real interest rate.

Price on the vertical axis: the real interest rate. A change in the rate moves along the curves; a change in saving or investment plans shifts them.

2. Key ideas

The market

Loanable funds market

Savers lend and borrowers borrow, usually through banks and bond markets. The price of a loan is the real interest rate. Equilibrium sets the real interest rate and the quantity of loanable funds.

Supply side

Supply of loanable funds

Supply comes from national saving plus foreign saving. More private saving, a bigger budget surplus, or an inflow of foreign financial capital shifts SLF right and lowers the real interest rate.

Demand side

Demand for loanable funds

Demand comes from firms that borrow to invest and from a government that borrows to cover a deficit. An investment tax credit or business optimism shifts DLF right and raises the real interest rate.

NS = private + public

National saving

Private saving is disposable income minus consumption. Public saving is the budget balance, T − G. A deficit is negative public saving, so it pulls national saving down.

Two ways to draw it

A budget deficit

Show a deficit as DLF shifting right (the government borrows) or as SLF shifting left (public saving falls). Both raise the real interest rate and lower private investment. AP accepts either if you label it.

Real vs. nominal

Not the money market

The loanable funds market sets the real interest rate and both curves slope. The money market sets the nominal interest rate and has a vertical money supply set by the central bank. Pick the graph that matches the question.

4. On the FRQ

How to earn the points

  • Label the vertical axis Real interest rate and the horizontal axis Quantity of loanable funds.
  • Draw SLF sloping up and DLF sloping down. Mark the starting equilibrium r₁ and Q₁ on the axes.
  • Draw the new curve, label it (DLF2 or SLF2), and mark the new r₂ and Q₂. Show r₂ clearly above or below r₁.
  • For a deficit, either shift is accepted. Pick one and stay consistent. Do not shift both curves for the same deficit.
  • When asked about investment, finish the chain in words: "The real interest rate rises, so private investment decreases."
  • For national saving, write the formula first: national saving = private saving + public saving = 200 + (−50) = 150.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up the two interest rate graphs. Loanable funds shows the real interest rate and an upward-sloping supply of saving. The money market shows the nominal interest rate and a vertical money supply. A question about saving, borrowing, or crowding out wants loanable funds.
  • Shifting supply right for a deficit. A deficit lowers public saving, so if you shift supply it goes left.
  • Saying private investment rises when households save less. Less saving raises the real interest rate, so investment falls.
  • Shifting a curve when the interest rate changes. A change in the real interest rate is a movement along both curves.
  • Calling public saving G − T. Public saving is T − G; a deficit makes it negative.
  • Stating both the rate and quantity when both curves shift. One of them is indeterminate unless the question gives sizes.
