The Money Market Study Lab

The money market is where the demand for money meets the supply of money set by the central bank, and together they determine the nominal interest rate. Shift money demand with the price level, real GDP, and payment technology, shift money supply with central bank policy, and watch the interest rate and bond prices respond. Then push the rate away from equilibrium and see how bond buying and selling pulls it back.

2. Key ideas

The market

Money market

Money demand (MD) and money supply (MS) meet at the equilibrium nominal interest rate. The vertical axis is the nominal interest rate because the cost of holding money is the interest you give up, and that is a nominal amount.

For spending

Transaction demand for money

People and firms hold money to buy things. When the price level rises, each purchase takes more dollars. When real GDP rises, there are more purchases. Either change raises money demand at every interest rate, so MD shifts right.

For storing wealth

Asset demand for money

People can hold wealth as money, which earns little or no interest, or as bonds, which pay interest. A higher nominal interest rate makes bonds more attractive, so people hold less money as an asset.

Downward slope

Why MD slopes down

The nominal interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding money. When the rate rises, holding money costs more, so the quantity of money demanded falls. A change in the interest rate is a movement along MD, not a shift.

Vertical MS

The central bank sets the money supply

MS is vertical because the quantity of money does not depend on the interest rate. In the limited-reserves model, buying bonds (open market purchases), lowering the discount rate, or lowering the reserve requirement shifts MS right. Topic 4.6 adds the ample-reserves tools, where the central bank steers rates with administered rates such as interest on reserves.

Inverse link

Bonds pull the rate to equilibrium

Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. Above equilibrium, people hold more money than they want and buy bonds, so bond prices rise and the rate falls. Below equilibrium, they sell bonds for cash, so bond prices fall and the rate rises.

4. Drawing it on the FRQ

Labels that earn points

  • Vertical axis: Nominal interest rate. Horizontal axis: Quantity of money.
  • Draw MS as a vertical line and MD sloping down. Label them MS and MD.
  • Mark the starting rate i₁ on the vertical axis and the starting quantity on the horizontal axis.
  • For a shift, draw MD₂ or MS₂, add an arrow, and mark the new rate i₂ above or below i₁.
  • When only MD shifts, the quantity of money stays the same. Do not mark a new quantity.
  • If asked about bonds, write it out: "The nominal interest rate rises, so the price of previously issued bonds falls."
  • Finish the chain when asked: a lower nominal interest rate raises interest-sensitive spending such as investment, so AD shifts right.

Common mistakes

  • Labeling the vertical axis "real interest rate." That label belongs on the loanable funds graph.
  • Drawing MS sloping up. In the money market it is vertical at the quantity the central bank sets.
  • Shifting MD when the interest rate changes. A rate change moves along MD.
  • Saying the quantity of money rises when MD shifts right. With a vertical MS, only the rate changes.
  • Getting disequilibrium backwards. Above equilibrium there is a surplus of money, so people buy bonds and the rate falls.
  • Saying bond prices and interest rates move together. They always move in opposite directions.
  • Treating "demand for money" as wanting more income. It means wanting to hold wealth as money instead of bonds.
