A change in the good's own price moves buyers along the demand curve, while a change in anything else that affects buyers shifts the whole curve. How to decide

  1. Find the good whose market you are graphing.
  2. Did that good's own price change? Then it is a movement along the demand curve. A higher price means up the curve (quantity demanded falls). A lower price means down the curve (quantity demanded rises).
  3. Did anything else change? Check the determinants: tastes, prices of related goods, income, number of buyers, expectations. Then the whole curve shifts. More wanted at every price is right; less is left.
  4. Related goods: a substitute's price and demand for this good move the same way. A complement's price and demand for this good move in opposite ways.
  5. Income: demand for a normal good moves the same way as income. Demand for an inferior good moves the opposite way.
  6. A change in supply changes the price, so on the demand side it is only a movement along the curve.

2. Key ideas

Two words, two meanings

Demand vs. quantity demanded

Demand is the whole curve: how much buyers want at every price. Quantity demanded is one point on it: how much they want at one price. A price change changes quantity demanded. A determinant changes demand.

Law of demand

Price and quantity move opposite

Other things equal, a higher price lowers quantity demanded and a lower price raises it, so the demand curve slopes down. How much quantity responds is called elasticity, which you study in microeconomics.

Why it slopes down

Three reasons

Substitution effect: a higher price makes other goods relatively cheaper, so buyers switch. Income effect: a higher price shrinks what your income can buy. Diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit adds less satisfaction, so buyers buy more only at a lower price.

TRIBE

Determinants of demand

Tastes, Related goods' prices, Income, number of Buyers, Expectations. A change in any of these shifts the curve. Right is an increase in demand; left is a decrease.

Income

Normal and inferior goods

For a normal good, higher income raises demand. For an inferior good, such as bus rides or store-brand ramen for many buyers, higher income lowers demand because buyers switch to something they like better.

Related goods

Substitutes and complements

Substitutes replace each other (burgers and pizza): a higher burger price raises demand for pizza. Complements go together (soda and pizza): a higher soda price lowers demand for pizza.

4. Drawing it on the FRQ

Labels that earn points

  • Vertical axis: Price (or P). Horizontal axis: Quantity (or Q). Write the good's name above the graph when the question names one.
  • Draw demand sloping down and label it D or D₁.
  • For a shift, draw the new curve, label it D₂, and add an arrow from D₁ to D₂.
  • Mark points on the axes: P₁ and Q₁ at the start, Q₂ (and P₂ if price changed) after.
  • In words, use the exact phrase: "demand increases" or "demand decreases" for a shift; "quantity demanded increases" or "decreases" for a price change.
  • When you explain, name the determinant: "Burgers are a substitute for pizza, so a higher burger price increases the demand for pizza."

Common mistakes

  • Writing "the price fell, so demand increased." A lower price raises quantity demanded. The curve stays put.
  • Shifting demand when supply changes. A supply shift changes the price, which only moves buyers along the demand curve.
  • Calling a shift "up" or "down." Say right (increase) or left (decrease).
  • Getting complements backward. A higher price for soda lowers the demand for pizza; it does not raise it.
  • Assuming higher income always raises demand. For an inferior good, demand falls.
  • Reversing expectations. If buyers expect a higher price later, they buy more now, so today's demand rises.
