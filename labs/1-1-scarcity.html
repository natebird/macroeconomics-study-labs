A. Sorting game: factors of production

How to decide

  1. Found in nature, not made by people (soil, water, minerals, forests, fish): land. Payment: rent.
  2. The time and effort people put into work: labor. Payment: wages.
  3. A human-made tool, machine, vehicle, or building used to make other goods: physical capital. Payment: interest.
  4. Knowledge, skills, and training that make a worker more productive: human capital.
  5. Taking a risk to organize the other factors and start or run a business: entrepreneurship. Payment: profit.
  6. Money, stocks, and bonds: financial capital. It buys resources but produces nothing by itself, so it is not a factor of production.

2. Key ideas

Always vs. sometimes

Scarcity vs. shortage

Scarcity is permanent: resources are limited and wants are not, so it applies to every economy and almost every good. A shortage is a temporary market condition where quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied at the current price. A higher price ends a shortage; nothing ends scarcity.

Land · labor · capital · entrepreneurship

Factors of production

The resources used to make goods and services. Land earns rent, labor earns wages, physical capital earns interest, and entrepreneurship earns profit. Human capital is the skill inside labor. In economics, capital means tools, machines, and buildings, not money.

Next best only

Trade-offs and opportunity cost

Every choice under scarcity is a trade-off. The opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the next best alternative you give up, not the total of every option you skipped. It includes money spent and the value of time. Choose an option when its benefit is at least its opportunity cost.

What · how · for whom

Three questions, three systems

Every society must decide what to produce, how to produce it, and who gets it. A market economy answers through prices and individual choices. A command economy answers through government planning. Real economies are mixed: mostly markets, with some government decisions.

Parts vs. whole

Microeconomics vs. macroeconomics

Micro studies individual households, firms, and single markets, such as the price of coffee or how many workers a bakery hires. Macro studies the whole economy: real GDP, the price level, inflation, unemployment, and growth.

Is vs. should

Positive vs. normative

A positive statement describes how the economy works and can be tested with evidence, even if it turns out false. A normative statement is a value judgment about what ought to be. Words like "should", "ought", "fair", "too high", and "better" signal normative.

4. Answering it on the exam

Moves that earn points

  • Define opportunity cost in these words: "the value of the next best alternative given up." Graders look for "next best".
  • When you compute an opportunity cost, include money spent plus the value of time given up, and show the addition, for example $30 ticket + $36 forgone wages = $66.
  • When a question says "capital" in a macro setting, read it as physical capital. An increase in physical capital or human capital raises a country's productive capacity.
  • When asked to identify a factor of production, use the exact AP term: land, labor, physical capital, human capital, or entrepreneurship.
  • To classify a statement, look for value words first. If it says "should" or "fair", it is normative. If it can be checked with data, it is positive.
  • Name the economic system by who decides: prices and individuals (market) or the government (command).

Common mistakes

  • Calling money "capital". A bank deposit or a stock is financial capital. It is not a factor of production.
  • Adding up every option you skipped. Opportunity cost is only the single best alternative you gave up.
  • Forgetting time. Something with a free ticket still has an opportunity cost: the best other use of those hours.
  • Using scarcity and shortage as the same word. A shortage disappears when the price rises to equilibrium; scarcity never disappears.
  • Thinking a positive statement must be true. "Tax cuts always raise tax revenue" is positive because evidence can test it.
  • Mixing up labor and human capital. The hours worked are labor; the skills that make those hours productive are human capital.
