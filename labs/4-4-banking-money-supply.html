2. Key ideas

Fractional reserves

Banks lend most deposits

A bank keeps only part of its deposits as reserves (vault cash plus its deposit at the Fed) and lends the rest. When a loan is spent and redeposited at another bank, that bank can lend again. This chain is how the banking system creates money.

Assets = liabilities + equity

The T-account

Assets go on the left: reserves, loans, and securities. Liabilities and owner's equity go on the right: demand deposits and the owners' capital. Deposits are a liability because the bank owes them to customers. Every move changes the two sides by the same amount, or swaps one asset for another.

rr × deposits

Required vs. excess reserves

Required reserves = required reserve ratio × demand deposits. Excess reserves = total reserves − required reserves. A single bank can safely lend only its excess reserves.

1 / rr

Money multiplier

The money multiplier is 1 divided by the required reserve ratio. With a 10% ratio it is 10. The maximum change in the money supply = excess reserves × money multiplier (for a Fed purchase from the public, add the initial deposit, which is itself new money). Do not mix it up with the spending multiplier, 1/MPS.

Where the reserves come from

Cash deposit vs. Fed purchase

Depositing cash you already had does not change the money supply at first: currency just turns into a checkable deposit. Only the new loans add money. When the Fed buys bonds from the public, it pays with brand-new money, so the deposit itself is new money and counts too.

Leakages

Why the real multiplier is smaller

The simple multiplier assumes banks lend every dollar of excess reserves and every loan comes back as a deposit. When banks hold extra reserves or people hold some cash, less is relent each round, so the actual multiplier is smaller than 1/rr.

4. Answering the FRQ

How to earn the points

  • Write the formula, then the numbers: "Required reserves = 0.10 × $1,000 = $100." Graders award calculation points only when the work is shown.
  • For one bank, the answer is its excess reserves. For the whole banking system, the answer is excess reserves × 1/rr.
  • Read who sold the bond. Fed buys from the public: max change in money supply = purchase × multiplier. Fed buys from a bank: the purchase adds that much to excess reserves, so the max change is also purchase × multiplier, but the money supply does not change until loans are made.
  • For a cash deposit, say the money supply does not change at the moment of the deposit because currency and demand deposits are both in M1. Then compute the loans: (1 − rr) × deposit × 1/rr.
  • In a T-account, put reserves, loans, and securities under Assets; put demand deposits and owner's equity under Liabilities. Show each entry as a + or − change.
  • Use the word "maximum" or "potential." The multiplier gives an upper limit because banks may hold excess reserves and people may hold cash.

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying the whole cash deposit by the multiplier when asked for the change in the money supply. That gives the change in demand deposits; the money supply change is excess reserves × multiplier.
  • Saying one bank can lend excess reserves × the multiplier. One bank can lend only its own excess reserves.
  • Counting reserves as part of the money supply. Reserves held by banks are not money in the hands of the public.
  • Calculating required reserves on a Fed purchase from a bank. No deposit changed, so required reserves do not change and the whole amount is excess reserves.
  • Listing deposits as an asset of the bank. Deposits are money the bank owes, so they are a liability.
  • Using 1/MPS. That is the spending multiplier from Unit 3. The money multiplier is 1/rr.
