2. Key ideas

Why it slopes up

Sticky wages and input prices

In the short run, nominal wages are fixed by contracts and norms, and some other input prices are slow to change. When the price level rises, firms get more for their output while costs stay the same. Profit per unit rises, so firms produce more real GDP.

Movement

Change in quantity supplied

A change in the price level moves the economy along the SRAS curve. A higher price level means more real GDP supplied; a lower price level means less. The curve itself does not move.

Shift

Change in SRAS

A change in anything that affects production costs at every price level shifts the whole curve. Higher per-unit costs shift SRAS left (a decrease). Lower per-unit costs shift it right (an increase).

Determinants

What shifts SRAS

Input prices such as wages and oil, productivity, business taxes and subsidies, government regulation, expected inflation, and supply shocks. Each one works through the cost of producing a unit of output.

Expectations

Expected inflation

If workers and firms expect higher inflation, workers ask for higher nominal wages and suppliers raise input prices now. Costs rise at every price level, so SRAS shifts left.

Stagflation

Negative supply shocks

When SRAS shifts left and AD does not change, the price level rises and real GDP falls at the same time. That mix of inflation and falling output is called stagflation, and the price rise is cost-push inflation.

4. Drawing it on the FRQ

Labels that earn points

  • Vertical axis: Price level (PL). Horizontal axis: Real GDP (or real output). Never "price" and "quantity".
  • Draw SRAS sloping upward and AD sloping downward. Label both curves.
  • Mark the starting equilibrium and drop dashed lines to both axes: PL₁ and Y₁.
  • For a shift, draw SRAS₂ to the correct side, add an arrow, and label the new PL₂ and Y₂ on the axes.
  • In words, name the determinant and the cost link: "Higher oil prices raise production costs, so SRAS shifts left; the price level rises and real GDP falls."

Common mistakes

  • Shifting SRAS when the price level changes. A price level change is a movement along SRAS.
  • Shifting SRAS "up" for a decrease and then drawing it to the right. A decrease in SRAS is a shift left (which also looks up).
  • Saying a negative supply shock lowers the price level. With AD unchanged, a left shift raises the price level and lowers real GDP.
  • Shifting SRAS for a change in consumer or government spending. Spending changes shift AD.
  • Calling a subsidy a cost. A subsidy lowers firms' net costs, so SRAS shifts right; a business tax shifts it left.
  • Mixing up SRAS and LRAS. Sticky wages explain the upward slope of SRAS; in the long run wages adjust and LRAS is vertical.
