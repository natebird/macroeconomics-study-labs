2. Key ideas

Definition

Exchange rate

The price of one currency in terms of another. "0.80 euros per dollar" means one dollar costs 0.80 euros. The currency after "per" is the one being priced.

1 ÷ rate

Reciprocal rates

Every rate can be written two ways, and the two numbers are reciprocals. If $1 = €0.80, then €1 = 1 ÷ 0.80 = $1.25. When one number goes up, the other goes down.

Stronger

Appreciation

A currency appreciates when one unit buys more of the other currency. If the rate rises from 1.00 to 1.25 euros per dollar, the dollar appreciated.

Weaker

Depreciation

A currency depreciates when one unit buys less of the other currency. When the dollar appreciates against the euro, the euro depreciates against the dollar. Both happen at once.

Trade

Exports and imports

An appreciating currency makes the country's goods more expensive to foreign buyers and foreign goods cheaper at home. Its exports fall and its imports rise. A depreciating currency does the opposite.

Who sets it

Floating vs. fixed

Under a floating exchange rate, supply and demand in the foreign exchange market set the rate. Under a fixed exchange rate, a government or central bank buys and sells currency to hold the rate at a target.

4. Writing it on the FRQ

Answers that earn points

  • Name the currency, the direction, and the other currency: "The dollar appreciates relative to the euro." "Stronger" alone may not earn the point.
  • Show the reciprocal as a step: €1 = 1 ÷ 0.80 = $1.25. Graders want to see the division.
  • Show conversions with units: $60 × 1.25 €/$ = €75. Keep the units so you can tell whether to multiply or divide.
  • For trade effects, give the price link and the result: "U.S. goods become more expensive for Europeans, so U.S. exports to Europe decrease."
  • If asked about net exports, finish it: exports fall and imports rise, so U.S. net exports decrease.
  • On a foreign exchange graph for dollars, label the vertical axis with the price of the dollar (for example, "Euros per dollar") and the horizontal axis "Quantity of dollars".

Common mistakes

  • Reading "dollars per euro rose" as the dollar getting stronger. That number is the price of a euro, so the euro appreciated.
  • Saying both currencies appreciated. If one currency appreciates against a second, the second depreciates against the first.
  • Assuming the two percent changes match. A move from 1.00 to 1.25 €/$ is a 25% appreciation of the dollar but a 20% depreciation of the euro.
  • Saying an appreciation helps a country's exporters. It hurts them, because their goods cost more abroad. It helps that country's importers and consumers of foreign goods.
  • Changing the seller's own price. A U.S. good still costs $60 in the U.S.; only its price in euros changes.
