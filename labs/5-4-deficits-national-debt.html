3. Key ideas

Flow

Budget deficit

The amount by which government spending (purchases plus transfer payments) exceeds tax revenue in a single year. The government borrows to cover it by selling bonds.

Stock

National debt

The total amount the government owes at a point in time. It equals the sum of all past deficits minus all past surpluses.

Flow

Budget surplus

Tax revenue is greater than spending for the year. The government can use the surplus to pay back bondholders, which reduces the debt.

Two kinds

Cyclical vs. structural deficit

A cyclical deficit comes from the business cycle: recessions shrink tax revenue and raise transfers. A structural deficit is the part that would remain even at full employment.

Built in

Automatic stabilizers

Progressive income taxes and transfer programs like unemployment insurance change on their own as the economy moves, with no new law. They push the budget toward deficit in recessions and toward surplus in expansions.

Creditors

Who holds the debt

Anyone who owns a government bond: households, banks, pension funds, foreign investors and governments, the central bank, and other government agencies.

Long run

Interest burden

A bigger debt means bigger interest payments. Interest is spending too, so it adds to future deficits and leaves less room for other programs.

Link to 5.5

Crowding out

Government borrowing increases the demand for loanable funds, which raises the real interest rate and reduces private investment. Less investment means slower growth of the capital stock.

Long run

Burden on future generations

Future taxpayers inherit the interest payments. If crowding out lowers investment, they also inherit a smaller capital stock and less productive capacity.

4. What happens to the deficit and the debt

The deficit is a speed. The debt is the distance traveled. Slowing down is not the same as going backward.

5. Answering it on the FRQ

Phrases that earn points

  • Define the deficit for one year: government spending minus tax revenue.
  • Expansionary fiscal policy (more spending or lower taxes) increases the deficit. Contractionary fiscal policy decreases it.
  • When asked what happens to the debt, say whether there is still a deficit. Any deficit, however small, adds to the debt.
  • Connect borrowing to the loanable funds market: demand for loanable funds increases, the real interest rate rises, and private investment falls.
  • Name automatic stabilizers by example: progressive income taxes, unemployment insurance. Say they work without new legislation.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up the deficit and the debt. One is a yearly flow, the other is a total stock.
  • Saying the debt falls when the deficit falls. The debt only falls when there is a surplus.
  • Thinking a balanced budget erases the debt. It keeps the debt from growing, but the old debt is still owed.
  • Confusing the budget deficit with a trade deficit. The trade deficit is about imports and exports (Unit 6).
  • Saying the Fed controls the budget. Congress and the President run fiscal policy. The Fed runs monetary policy.
