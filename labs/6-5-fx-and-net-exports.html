2. Key ideas

Depreciation

A weaker dollar helps exports

When the dollar depreciates, each dollar buys fewer euros. U.S. goods get cheaper for Europeans, so U.S. exports rise. European goods get more expensive for Americans, so U.S. imports fall. Net exports increase.

Appreciation

A stronger dollar helps imports

When the dollar appreciates, U.S. goods get more expensive abroad and foreign goods get cheaper at home. U.S. exports fall, imports rise, and net exports decrease.

AD = C + I + G + Xn

Net exports shift AD

Net exports are a component of aggregate demand. A depreciation raises net exports and shifts AD right, so real GDP and the price level rise in the short run. An appreciation shifts AD left, so both fall.

Balance of payments

The current account moves with net exports

The balance of trade (net exports of goods and services) is the largest part of the current account. A depreciation moves the current account toward surplus. Because the balance of payments sums to zero, the capital and financial account moves by the same amount in the other direction.

Two currencies

One rises, the other falls

If the dollar appreciates against the euro, the euro depreciates against the dollar. Europe's net exports to the U.S. then rise while U.S. net exports fall.

Shift vs. slide

Not the foreign purchases effect

A change in the U.S. price level changes net exports and moves the economy along AD. A change in the exchange rate from some other cause, like interest rates or asset preferences, shifts the whole AD curve.

4. Drawing it on the FRQ

Labels that earn points

  • Market for dollars: horizontal axis Quantity of dollars, vertical axis the price of the dollar in the other currency, such as Euros per dollar. Label the curves D$ and S$.
  • Show the new curve with an arrow and mark the old and new exchange rates on the vertical axis (e₁ and e₂).
  • Use the words appreciates or depreciates. "The dollar gets stronger" may not earn the point.
  • Finish the chain in words: the dollar appreciates, so U.S. exports decrease and imports increase, so net exports decrease, so AD decreases.
  • On the AD–AS graph, label the axes Real GDP and Price level, draw LRAS at Yf, shift AD, and mark the new real GDP and price level (Y₂, PL₂).

Common mistakes

  • Reversing the export effect. A depreciation makes exports cheaper to foreigners, so exports rise.
  • Labeling the vertical axis "Price" or "Exchange rate" alone. Say whose currency is priced in which: "Euros per dollar".
  • Shifting both curves when one is enough. If the question only says foreigners want more U.S. assets, shift demand for dollars only.
  • Calling the exchange rate effect on AD a movement along AD. A price-level change slides along AD; an exchange rate change from another cause shifts AD.
  • Forgetting the other currency. If the dollar appreciates against the yen, the yen depreciates against the dollar.
  • Saying the current account and the capital and financial account move together. They move in opposite directions.
