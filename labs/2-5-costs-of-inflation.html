2. Key ideas

Purchasing power

Inflation erodes money's value

When the price level rises, each dollar buys fewer goods and services. Cash and any income fixed in dollars, such as a pension with no cost-of-living adjustment, lose purchasing power whether or not the inflation was expected.

Expected vs. surprise

Anticipated vs. unanticipated inflation

Anticipated inflation can be built into nominal interest rates and wage contracts, so it causes little redistribution. Unanticipated inflation catches people locked into contracts at the wrong rate, and purchasing power moves from one side of the contract to the other.

r = i − π

Borrowers vs. lenders

A fixed nominal rate includes the inflation lenders expected. If actual inflation is higher, the real rate falls: borrowers repay with dollars that buy less, so borrowers gain and lenders lose. If inflation is lower than expected, or prices fall, the reverse happens.

Real wage ≈ % ΔW − π

Real wages and real income

Real income is what your income can buy. A worker whose raise was fixed using expected inflation loses real income when inflation comes in higher, and the employer gains. A cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) raises pay with the price index and protects the real wage.

Even if anticipated

Shoe-leather and menu costs

Shoe-leather costs are the time and effort people spend holding less cash, such as extra trips to the bank. Menu costs are the real costs firms pay to change listed prices, such as reprinting menus and price tags. Both grow as inflation rises.

Measuring stick

Unit-of-account costs

Money is the unit we measure value in. Inflation makes that measure less reliable, which distorts decisions. Taxes are a key example: people can pay tax on nominal gains that are not real gains. In hyperinflation all of these costs become severe.

4. Writing it on the FRQ

Answers that earn points

  • Name the direction and give the reason in one sentence: "Unanticipated inflation helps borrowers with fixed-rate loans because they repay with dollars that have less purchasing power."
  • Show the real interest rate work: real rate = nominal rate − inflation. A 7% loan with 5% inflation has a real rate of 7 − 5 = 2%.
  • Show the real wage work the same way: % change in real wage ≈ % change in nominal wage − inflation rate. A 4% raise with 6% inflation is a real wage change of about −2%.
  • Use the word unanticipated (or "unexpected") when you explain redistribution. Graders look for the link between expectations and contracts.
  • If asked about anticipated inflation, say it still has costs: shoe-leather costs, menu costs, and unit-of-account costs.

Common mistakes

  • Saying inflation hurts everyone. Borrowers with fixed-rate loans and employers paying fixed wages gain from unanticipated inflation.
  • Saying higher prices always cut real income. If nominal income rises as fast as prices, real income is unchanged.
  • Forgetting that the results flip when inflation is lower than expected or when prices fall. Deflation helps lenders and hurts borrowers.
  • Mixing up shoe-leather costs (people holding less cash) with menu costs (firms changing prices).
  • Treating a COLA or a variable-rate loan as exposed. Both adjust with inflation, so they protect against surprises.
  • Confusing a high price level with inflation. Inflation is the rate at which the price level rises.
