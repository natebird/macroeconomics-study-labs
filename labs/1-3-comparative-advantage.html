    2. Key ideas

    More or faster

    Absolute advantage

    A producer has an absolute advantage in a good if it can make more of it with the same resources (output problem) or make one unit with fewer resources (input problem). One country can have an absolute advantage in both goods.

    Lower opportunity cost

    Comparative advantage

    A producer has a comparative advantage in a good if it gives up less of the other good to make one unit. Unless the opportunity costs are equal, each country has a comparative advantage in exactly one of the two goods.

    Output: other over

    Output problems

    The table shows how many units each country can produce. Opportunity cost of one good = the other good's output ÷ this good's output. If A makes 40 wheat or 20 cloth, 1 wheat costs 20 ÷ 40 = 0.5 cloth.

    Input: in on top

    Input problems

    The table shows the hours (or workers, or acres) needed to make one unit. Opportunity cost of one good = the input for the good you are asking about ÷ the other good's input. If a shirt takes 6 hours and a hat takes 3, 1 shirt costs 6 ÷ 3 = 2 hats. Lower numbers mean absolute advantage here.

    The trade price

    Terms of trade

    The terms of trade are how much of one good trades for one unit of the other. Both countries gain only if the terms fall between their two opportunity costs. Outside that range, one country could do better by making the good itself.

    Why comparative

    Gains from trade

    Trade lets each country get a good for less than it would cost to make at home. What matters is the opportunity cost each country faces, which comparative advantage measures. Production stays on each PPC, but consumption moves outside it.

    4. Answering it on the FRQ

    What graders want to see

    • Write the opportunity cost with the numbers and units: "A's opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 20 cloth ÷ 40 wheat = 0.5 units of cloth."
    • Justify comparative advantage with a comparison of opportunity costs: "A has a comparative advantage in wheat because its opportunity cost of wheat (0.5 cloth) is lower than B's (1.5 cloth)."
    • Justify absolute advantage with the raw numbers: "A can produce more wheat (40 > 10)" or "A needs fewer hours per unit (2 < 6)."
    • For terms of trade, state the range and check your answer against both opportunity costs: "1 wheat for 1 cloth benefits both because 0.5 < 1 < 1.5."
    • On a PPC, put one good on each axis, label the intercepts, and draw a straight line for constant opportunity cost. The slope's size is the opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good.
    • For gains from trade, say consumption moves to a point outside the PPC. Production stays on the PPC.

    Common mistakes

    • Using the output formula on an input table. In an input problem, the good you are asking about goes on top.
    • Calling the larger number in an input table an absolute advantage. More hours per unit means slower, so the smaller number wins.
    • Deciding specialization by absolute advantage. A country with an absolute advantage in both goods still specializes only in its comparative advantage.
    • Saying one country has a comparative advantage in both goods. With two goods, a lower cost of one good always means a higher cost of the other.
    • Choosing terms of trade outside the range between the two opportunity costs, or exactly at one end (that country gains nothing).
    • Dropping units. "0.5" earns less than "0.5 units of cloth."
