Shifts in the Foreign Exchange Market Study Lab

The value of the dollar moves when something changes how many dollars foreigners want to buy or how many dollars Americans want to sell. Relative interest rates, relative price levels, relative incomes, tastes, expectations, and government policy all shift those curves. Pick an event, watch which curve shifts in the market for dollars, and follow the chain from the cause to appreciation or depreciation.

    2. Key ideas

    Two sides

    Who buys and who sells dollars

    Foreigners demand dollars to buy U.S. exports, U.S. stocks and bonds, and U.S. real assets. Americans supply dollars when they trade them for foreign currency to buy imports and foreign assets. Ask who is trading and which way, then shift that curve.

    Capital flows

    Relative interest rates

    A higher U.S. real interest rate relative to rates abroad makes U.S. financial assets more attractive. Foreign financial capital flows in, demand for dollars rises, and the dollar appreciates. A lower relative rate does the reverse.

    Trade flows

    Relative prices and incomes

    Higher U.S. inflation relative to abroad makes U.S. goods pricier, so demand for dollars falls and supply rises: the dollar depreciates. Higher U.S. income means more imports, so supply of dollars rises and the dollar depreciates. Higher foreign income means more U.S. exports and a stronger dollar.

    Demand side

    Tastes, expectations, speculation

    If foreigners come to prefer U.S. goods, demand for dollars rises. If traders expect the dollar to appreciate later, speculators buy dollars now, which raises demand and appreciates the dollar today.

    Policy

    Monetary and fiscal policy

    Contractionary monetary policy and deficit-financed expansionary fiscal policy both raise the U.S. real interest rate, attract foreign financial capital, and appreciate the dollar. Expansionary monetary policy lowers the interest rate and depreciates the dollar.

    Mirror image

    One rises, the other falls

    If the dollar appreciates against the euro, the euro depreciates against the dollar. In the market for euros, an increase in demand for dollars shows up as an increase in the supply of euros, because Europeans sell euros to get dollars.

    4. Drawing it on the FRQ

    Labels that earn points

    • Title the graph Market for U.S. dollars. Horizontal axis: Quantity of dollars. Vertical axis: the price of the dollar in the other currency, such as Euros per dollar.
    • Label the curves D$ and S$. Draw the new curve, label it D$₂ or S$₂, and add an arrow for the direction of the shift.
    • Mark the starting exchange rate e₁ and the new rate e₂ on the vertical axis. A higher e₂ means the dollar appreciated.
    • Write the chain in words: "The U.S. real interest rate rises, so foreigners demand more U.S. financial assets, so demand for dollars increases and the dollar appreciates."
    • If asked about the other currency, say it does the opposite. If asked to graph it, the euro market uses "Dollars per euro" on the vertical axis.
    • Linked questions often start in the money market or loanable funds market. Show the interest rate change there first, then carry it to the foreign exchange market.

    Common mistakes

    • Labeling the vertical axis "Price" or "Exchange rate" with no currency. Graders want the price of one currency in the other, such as euros per dollar.
    • Saying higher U.S. income makes the dollar stronger. Higher U.S. income raises U.S. imports, so Americans supply more dollars and the dollar depreciates.
    • Shifting a curve because the exchange rate changed. A change in the exchange rate is a movement along the curves. Only a determinant shifts them.
    • Mixing up the two markets. More demand for dollars from Europeans means more supply of euros in the euro market, not more demand for euros.
    • Calling quantity certain when both curves shift. With demand left and supply right, the dollar surely depreciates, but the change in quantity of dollars is indeterminate.
    • Saying expansionary monetary policy appreciates the dollar. It lowers the interest rate, so financial capital flows out and the dollar depreciates.
