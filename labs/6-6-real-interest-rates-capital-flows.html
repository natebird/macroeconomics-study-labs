2. Key ideas

The rule

Capital chases the higher real rate

Savers want the best return. When one country's real interest rate rises relative to another's, financial capital flows into the country with the higher rate. The gap between the two rates is the real interest rate differential.

Loanable funds

Inflows add to supply

A capital inflow increases the supply of loanable funds in the country receiving it, which pulls its real interest rate back down. The country the money leaves sees its supply of loanable funds decrease, so its real interest rate rises.

Foreign exchange

To buy U.S. assets, buy dollars

Foreigners need dollars to buy U.S. bonds. A capital inflow increases the demand for dollars, so the dollar appreciates. A capital outflow increases the supply of dollars (or decreases demand), so the dollar depreciates.

Net exports

A stronger dollar hurts exports

When the dollar appreciates, U.S. goods cost more for foreigners and foreign goods cost less for Americans. Exports fall, imports rise, and net exports decrease. A weaker dollar does the reverse.

Balance of payments

Twin deficits

The current account and the capital and financial account sum to zero. A larger capital inflow (financial account surplus) is matched by a larger current account deficit. That is why a budget deficit can lead to a trade deficit.

Policy

Monetary policy moves capital too

Expansionary monetary policy lowers interest rates in the short run. Lower U.S. rates send financial capital abroad, the dollar depreciates, and net exports rise. Contractionary policy does the opposite.

4. Drawing it on the FRQ

Labels that earn points

  • Loanable funds: vertical axis Real interest rate, horizontal axis Quantity of loanable funds. Label SLF and DLF, then r₁ and r₂ on the vertical axis.
  • Foreign exchange: horizontal axis Quantity of dollars, vertical axis the price of the dollar, such as Euros per dollar. Label S$ and D$.
  • Write the chain in order: the real interest rate changes, capital flows in or out, demand for (or supply of) the currency shifts, the currency appreciates or depreciates, net exports change.
  • Name the direction of the flow with the country: "financial capital flows into the United States." Say "relative to" the other country's rate.
  • If the question asks about loanable funds after the flow, an inflow shifts the U.S. supply of loanable funds right and the other country's supply left.
  • For the market for euros, flip everything: the axis is "Dollars per euro", and a U.S. inflow means the supply of euros increases and the euro depreciates.

Common mistakes

  • Sending capital toward the lower rate. Money flows to the country with the higher real interest rate.
  • Shifting the supply of dollars right for an inflow. Foreigners buying U.S. assets demand dollars, so D$ shifts right. (A leftward shift of S$, as Americans keep saving at home, is also accepted.) Americans buying foreign assets supply dollars.
  • Saying net exports rise when the dollar appreciates. A stronger dollar makes U.S. exports more expensive, so net exports fall.
  • Ignoring the real rate. Capital flows respond to the real interest rate. If a question starts in the money market, note that with sticky prices the nominal rate change is also a real rate change in the short run, then carry it to the foreign exchange market.
  • Forgetting the second country. An inflow to the U.S. is an outflow from Europe, which reduces Europe's supply of loanable funds and raises its real interest rate.
  • Mixing up the accounts. A capital inflow is a surplus in the capital and financial account, matched by a deficit in the current account.
