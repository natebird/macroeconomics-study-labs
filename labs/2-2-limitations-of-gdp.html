Limitations of GDP Study Lab

GDP measures the market value of final goods and services produced inside a country in a year. It leaves out some production, like unpaid housework and the underground economy, and it says nothing about leisure, pollution, or who gets the income. Sort items into "counts" or "does not count," then compare two countries with the same GDP per capita to see what the number hides.

How to decide

  1. Is it a new final good or service produced this year? If it was made in an earlier year (a used car), it was already counted then.
  2. Was it produced inside U.S. borders? GDP is about location, not who owns the firm.
  3. Was it sold in a market and reported? Unpaid work at home and hidden cash or illegal sales are left out.
  4. Is it only money or ownership changing hands? Stock purchases and transfer payments (Social Security) buy no new output.
  5. Is it an intermediate good used up in making something else? Count only the final good, or you count the input twice.
  6. If it counts, name the part: C (household purchases), I (new capital, new houses, inventory), G (government purchases), or exports in net exports.

2. Key ideas

Left out: unpaid work

Nonmarket activities

Cooking at home, cleaning, caring for your own children, and volunteering are real production, but nobody pays a market price for them. GDP leaves them out, so it understates total production. Hire a cleaner for the same work and GDP rises.

Left out: hidden sales

Underground economy

Illegal sales and legal work paid in unreported cash never reach government records. GDP misses them, so it understates production most in countries with large underground economies.

Free time

Leisure

GDP counts what people produce, not the time they spend producing it. If a country works longer hours to reach the same GDP, its people have less free time, and GDP per capita does not show that loss.

Better and new goods

Quality changes

A phone that costs the same as last year's but does far more adds about the same to GDP. Measured GDP does not fully capture gains in quality or the arrival of new goods, so it can understate improvements in living standards.

Who gets it

Distribution of income

GDP per capita is an average. Two countries with the same average can have very different incomes for the typical family if one country's income goes mostly to the top earners.

Side effects

Externalities and the environment

GDP does not subtract the harm from pollution or the use of natural resources. Spending to clean up pollution adds to GDP, even though it only repairs damage. GDP can rise while the environment gets worse.

4. Answering it on the FRQ

Moves that earn points

  • When asked if something counts, give a yes or no and then the reason in one sentence, such as "No, a used car was counted in GDP the year it was produced."
  • If it counts, name the component: consumption, investment, government purchases, or net exports. New houses and unsold inventory are investment.
  • For a GDP calculation, write GDP = C + I + G + (X − M) first, then plug in only the items that belong. Cross out transfers, used goods, stock purchases, and intermediate goods.
  • For "why is GDP an imperfect measure of well-being," name a specific omission: leisure, income distribution, pollution and other externalities, nonmarket production, the underground economy, or quality changes.
  • Say the direction: nonmarket and underground activity make GDP understate production. Pollution cleanup makes GDP rise without making people better off than before the damage.
  • To compare living standards across countries, use real GDP per capita: real GDP divided by population.

Common mistakes

  • Counting a stock or bond purchase as investment. In GDP, investment means new capital goods, new houses, and inventory changes.
  • Counting Social Security or unemployment checks as government purchases. They are transfer payments.
  • Leaving out a foreign-owned factory inside the U.S. GDP counts production by location.
  • Thinking unsold goods do not count. They count as inventory investment in the year they are made.
  • Adding the price of the tires and the price of the car. Count only the final good, or use value added at each stage.
  • Saying higher GDP per capita always means people are better off. It ignores leisure, distribution, and the environment.
