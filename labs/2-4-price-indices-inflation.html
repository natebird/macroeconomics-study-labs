2. Key ideas

Price index

Base year = 100

A price index compares the cost of the same market basket in each year with its cost in the base year. In the base year the index is always 100. An index of 125 means the basket costs 25% more than in the base year.

CPI vs. deflator

Two ways to measure the price level

The CPI prices a fixed basket bought by a typical urban household, including imports. The GDP deflator covers all final goods and services produced in the country: nominal GDP ÷ real GDP × 100. It leaves out imports and has no fixed basket.

Inflation rate

Inflation, deflation, disinflation

Inflation is a rising price level (positive inflation rate). Deflation is a falling price level (negative rate). Disinflation is a falling inflation rate while prices still rise, such as 6% down to 2%.

Biases

Why the CPI overstates inflation

Substitution bias: people switch away from goods that get pricier, but the basket stays fixed. New goods: new products enter the basket late. Quality changes: some price increases pay for better products, not just higher prices.

Nominal vs. real

Dollars vs. purchasing power

Nominal values are in current dollars. Real values remove price changes so you can compare what money buys across years. If your nominal wage rises 3% and prices rise 5%, your real wage falls about 2%.

Causes

Demand-pull and cost-push

Demand-pull inflation comes from aggregate demand rising faster than output can keep up. Cost-push inflation comes from higher production costs, such as wages or oil, that shift short-run aggregate supply left.

4. On the FRQ

How to show your work

  • Write the formula first, then plug in: "CPI = (cost in Year 2 ÷ cost in base year) × 100 = (220 ÷ 200) × 100 = 110." Graders award the point for the setup and the answer.
  • Find the cost of the basket by multiplying each base-year quantity by that year's price, then add. Use the same quantities every year.
  • For the inflation rate, divide by the old (earlier) index, not the new one or 100.
  • State the sign and name it: a positive rate is inflation, a negative rate is deflation, and a smaller positive rate than last year is disinflation.
  • For real values, label the answer "in base-year dollars." If asked whether purchasing power rose, compare the real values, or compare nominal growth with the inflation rate.

Common mistakes

  • Calling disinflation "deflation." If prices still rise, only more slowly, it is disinflation.
  • Reading the CPI as the inflation rate. A CPI of 110 means prices are 10% above the base year, not that inflation this year was 10%.
  • Subtracting CPIs and stopping: 126 − 120 = 6 points, but the inflation rate is 6 ÷ 120 = 5%.
  • Changing the quantities each year. The CPI holds the basket fixed; that is exactly why substitution bias happens.
  • Saying the CPI understates inflation. The standard biases (substitution, new goods, quality) make it overstate the rise in the cost of living.
